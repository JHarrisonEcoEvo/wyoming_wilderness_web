> "The Cirque of the Towers is one of the great gifts of the mountain gods"
>
> — Steve Bechtel

A fellow by the name of Orrin Bonney gave the Cirque of the Towers its English name in 1941. He and a few others climbed Pingora peak (1940), the anchor to the scenery of this magnificent valley. Since those days this area has attracted every generation of alpine climbers.

The rock is solid granite, something around 2–3 billion years old according to what I can find. It is a joy to touch and climb upon. Since the rock was exposed glaciers have had their way with the terrain, crafting an immensity.

![Pingora Peak](/images/wilderness/popo_agie/IMG_6502.jpg)
*Pingora Peak stands on reader's right*

> The evening before the climb, while camped at Lonesome Lake, Frank Garnick remarked, "I know some day some damn fool will climb it." The next day he and his two companions did climb it.
>
>—Mae Urbanek

The Popo Agie Wilderness holds the Cirque along with about twenty other peaks crossing the 12,000 foot high water mark. Wind River Peak is the tallest of these.

The Wilderness was designated in 1984. Like Cloud Peak, and other mountainous areas of Wyoming, much of the area was protected as a primitive area since the 1930s. During the initial Wilderness designation, Wyoming voiced its disapproval by passing a joint congressional memorial requesting no more Wilderness. The Senate Majority Leader at the time Don Jewitt (R) is quoted as saying, ["I think we now have wilderness areas up to our necks."](https://www.hcn.org/issues/5.12/). I found this statement in a 1973 issue of High Country News from Lander, Wy.

![Popo Agie To Be Considered](/images/wilderness/popo_agie/hcn-popo-agie-to-be-considered.png)
*High Country News, June 8, 1973*

Thankfully, this landscape was indeed protected as part of the Wyoming Wilderness Act bill of 1984, as championed by [Sen. Malcolm Wallop, with Alan Simpson and Dick Cheney co-sponsoring](https://www.congress.gov/bill/98th-congress/senate-bill/543).
 
### What does Popo Agie mean? Where does the water go?
Pronounced 'puh-PO-zhuh', this is the Crow name for these lands. Some sources I have found translate this as an onomatopoeia—the plopping river. The river descends from the shoulders of Wind River Peak into Sinks Canyon.

Sinks Canyon is a delightful drive or bike ride. I biked it once at the end of the 131 mile sufferfest—the 'Wyo 131' gravel bike ride. I had not been riding much that year, so my knees were unwilling to perform their one single task: bending. I coasted what must have been a dozen miles or more down Sinks Canyon. Perhaps the only longer coast I have had on a bike was in the Himalayas. I rode from around 17,000 feet or so down a very long series of jeep tracks and yak trails. I must have had a 30+ mile descent. Driving up canyon is a common way to approach the Wilderness. Along the way you will see the eponymous feature—the earth's maw swallowing the plopping river.

When the water is high, I don't like standing at the entrance of the sink; this place of down-going and rushing.

![Panorama](/images/wilderness/popo_agie/PANO_20180503_182636.jpg)

The water disappears for 3,500 feet before welling back up into a pool with dinosaur-sized trout. The trout bathe in more water than went underground to begin with, and warmer water too. It takes about two hours for the water to make its way back to the sunshine—according to a [dye study](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/use-dye-tracing-water-resources-investigations-wyoming-1967-94). 84% of the dye was recovered. You can take spelunking tours here. Wyoming PBS has some [great footage from the area](https://www.pbs.org/video/sinks-canyon-underground-mysteries-wlqhai/).

### Pressure

> The area is so large and so rough that there is little likelihood of its ever being crowded. Since it forms a part of the large Wind River Wilderness (Bridger, Glacier and Indian area), an area equal in size to Yosemite National Park and can be used only by those visitors who are able and willing to make long, hard trips with pack outfit, the area should meet all demands for many years to come.
>
> — The U.S. Forest Service circa the 1930s

You can find plenty of solitude here, but portions of the Wilderness are heavily used. 
Lonesome Lake was flagged for fecal contamination in 2022 by the EPA. This lovely lake, nestled in the cirque, had the highest *Enterococci* concentrations of any of the 980 lakes they surveyed. This was one sample; and maybe they just caught a brown trout, so to speak, because later on the Wyoming DEQ found very little *E. coli*. The DEQ sampled over a few months (from July to Sept) and took a few [samples](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1id1y2tZ0f0iwWbPNOr-6yP5HjuopyWBt/view?pli=1).

Whether the area has troubled water may depend on your timing. But it certainly has been heavily impacted. Practice Leave No Trace here. Packing out the poo is the best policy—as inconvenient as it is.

## Access

Big Sandy Lake Trailhead is the popular way to reach the Cirque. It is a long way from anywhere. To get here you may drive through Farson, Wyoming. If you do, stop at the 'home of the big scoop'. It is not the highest quality ice cream in the world, but it is good and the portions are ludicrous. A kid's cone is enough. The full size is like an entire carton of ice cream with a cone stuck to it.

Other access points can be found off Sinks Canyon road, Worthen Meadow, and Dickinson Park.

## Safety

The usual mountain hazards, exacerbated by the remote nature of these peaks. Some grizzly live here—so be bear aware. Polluted water could be an issue.

## Infrastructure

Plenty of trails. Trailheads often have equestrian facilities and bathrooms.
